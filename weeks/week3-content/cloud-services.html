Overview

IaaS, PaaS and SaaS represent different divisions of responsibility between a cloud provider and its customer (Mell and Grance, 2011). Moving from IaaS towards SaaS generally reduces the customer's infrastructure- management burden but also reduces direct technical control. It does not remove customer responsibility for identity, data governance, configuration and supplier assurance.

The most suitable model depends on technical expertise, regulatory requirements, application design, desired control and acceptable dependency on the provider.

Infrastructure as a Service

IaaS offers virtual servers, storage and networking. Customers can choose operating systems, install software and design detailed network configurations.

Benefits

  • high control over operating systems and applications;
  • supports custom and legacy software;
  • resources can be scaled without purchasing hardware;
  • useful for disaster recovery and temporary workloads.

Limitations

  • requires system-administration expertise;
  • the customer remains responsible for patching;
  • incorrect firewall or identity settings create risk;
  • cost can increase when resources are left running.

Suitable Organisational Use Case

IaaS can be appropriate for an organisation migrating an existing business application that requires a particular operating system and network design. It provides cloud flexibility without forcing immediate redesign. However, retaining a legacy architecture may also retain its operational weaknesses, so migration should not be treated as modernisation by itself.

Platform as a Service

PaaS provides managed application infrastructure. The development team focuses on code and data while the provider manages servers, operating systems and much of the runtime environment.

Benefits

  • reduces infrastructure-management work;
  • supports rapid development and deployment;
  • often includes scaling, logging and deployment tools;
  • standardises the application environment.

Limitations

  • applications must follow platform restrictions;
  • moving to another provider may require redesign;
  • provider outages affect hosted applications;
  • runtime versions may be controlled by the provider.

Suitable Organisational Use Case

PaaS is suitable for a software-development team creating web applications or APIs. It allows the team to deploy frequently without maintaining operating systems and server clusters. The productivity benefit must be weighed against platform constraints, service deprecation and the cost of redesigning provider-specific integrations.

Software as a Service

SaaS provides a complete application managed by the provider. Users normally access the software through a browser, mobile application or desktop client.

Benefits

  • rapid adoption;
  • minimal local infrastructure;
  • updates are handled by the provider;
  • subscription pricing may reduce initial cost;
  • supports access from different locations.

Limitations

  • limited technical customisation;
  • dependence on the provider and internet access;
  • data location may create compliance concerns;
  • subscription costs can accumulate over time;
  • service changes may be outside customer control.

Suitable Organisational Use Case

SaaS is appropriate for an organisation requiring standard email, file sharing, accounting or customer management. The organisation can use the application without maintaining the complete technical stack. Nevertheless, it must assess data location, contractual controls, identity security, export capability and business continuity before relying on the service.

Organisational Evaluation

Regulated Organisation

A regulated organisation may prefer IaaS or a carefully selected PaaS where it requires detailed control over network configuration, encryption, audit records and data location. However, additional control also creates additional responsibility. Regulation does not automatically require IaaS: a well-assured SaaS service may be preferable when it provides stronger controls than the customer could operate independently.

Software Start-Up

A start-up may benefit from PaaS because it can develop and deploy products quickly. The main risk is becoming dependent on provider-specific services that are difficult to replace later.

Small Non-Technical Organisation

A small organisation with limited technical staff is likely to benefit from SaaS. Security still requires strong account management, multifactor authentication and appropriate data-sharing settings.

Critical Evaluation

IaaS provides flexibility but can expose organisations to configuration and patch-management failures. PaaS reduces operational work, although platform dependence can limit portability. SaaS offers the simplest user experience but provides the least technical control.

The decision should therefore be based on organisational capability rather than only cost. Selecting a highly controllable service is not beneficial when the organisation lacks the staff or processes to manage it securely.

Organisations may use all three models. SaaS can support standard business functions, PaaS can support new application development and IaaS can host systems with specialised requirements.
