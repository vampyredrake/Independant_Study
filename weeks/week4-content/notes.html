Introduction

Artificial intelligence refers to computer systems that perform tasks associated with human intelligence, such as recognising patterns, making predictions, interpreting language and selecting actions. Modern AI systems are commonly based on machine learning, where models identify patterns from data rather than relying only on rules written directly by a programmer.

AI can improve efficiency and support decision-making, but its use also creates ethical and professional responsibilities. Developers must consider data quality, privacy, fairness, explainability, security and the consequences of incorrect outputs. NIST treats these characteristics as interdependent elements of trustworthy AI rather than optional additions applied after development (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

1. Artificial Intelligence

Definition and Scope

Artificial intelligence is a broad field concerned with developing computer systems that can perform tasks involving perception, reasoning, learning or decision-making.

Examples include recommendation systems, speech recognition, fraud detection, computer vision, route-planning software and automated language systems.

Narrow AI

Narrow AI is designed for a defined task or limited group of tasks. A recommendation model may predict products that a user is likely to select, but it does not possess general intelligence.

Existing production AI systems should normally be understood as specialised systems.

Artificial General Intelligence

Artificial general intelligence would be able to perform a wide range of intellectual tasks with adaptability comparable to a human.

Fluent output or broad task coverage does not establish human-like understanding or autonomy (Russell and Norvig, 2021).

Rule-Based Systems and Machine Learning

Rule-Based Systems

A rule-based system follows explicit conditions written by developers.

Rules are normally transparent and predictable, but may become difficult to maintain when a problem contains many exceptions.

Machine Learning

A machine learning system develops a mathematical model from examples.

It can identify complex patterns that are difficult to express as fixed rules, although the resulting behaviour may be harder to explain.

Key takeaway

Artificial intelligence is a broad discipline. Machine learning is one approach within AI rather than a synonym for the entire field.

2. Types of Machine Learning

Supervised Learning

Supervised learning uses labelled training data. Each example contains input values and a known output.

It includes:

  • Regression: predicts continuous values.
  • Classification: predicts categories.

Its main limitation is the need for reliable labelled data. Labels can be expensive, incomplete or biased (Géron, 2023).

Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised learning uses data without known output labels and attempts to identify structure or unusual patterns.

  • clustering;
  • dimensionality reduction;
  • anomaly detection.

Discovered patterns may not always have a useful interpretation, and evaluation can be difficult without a known correct answer.

Reinforcement Learning

Reinforcement learning involves an agent interacting with an environment and receiving rewards or penalties.

Applications include robotics, game-playing, resource allocation and automated control.

Poorly designed rewards may encourage unintended behaviour because the agent optimises the formal reward rather than the designer's unstated intention (Russell and Norvig, 2021).

Learning Type Training Signal Typical Tasks Main Limitation
Supervised Labelled examples Classification and regression Requires appropriate labelled data
Unsupervised No known output labels Clustering and anomaly detection Results may be difficult to interpret
Reinforcement Rewards and penalties Control, robotics and game-playing Reward design and training cost

Key takeaway

The appropriate learning approach depends on the available data, the form of the required output and whether feedback can be provided during training.

3. Machine-Learning Approaches

Linear Regression

Models the relationship between input variables and a continuous target using a linear function.

It is useful for simple forecasting and interpretation, but cannot represent complex non-linear relationships without additional feature engineering.

Logistic Regression

Estimates the probability that an example belongs to a class and can use a threshold to produce a final decision.

It is efficient and comparatively interpretable, but may struggle with complex decision boundaries.

Decision Trees

Divide data using a sequence of conditions. Branches represent outcomes and leaf nodes provide predictions.

Trees are easy to visualise but deep trees may overfit the training data.

Support Vector Machines

Attempt to identify a decision boundary that separates classes with the largest possible margin.

Kernel functions support non-linear classification, although training can become expensive on very large datasets.

Neural Networks

Contain connected layers of artificial neurons whose weighted connections are adjusted during training.

Deep networks can model complex patterns in images, sound and language, but often require significant data and computing resources.

Approach Typical Output Strength Limitation
Linear regression Continuous value Simple and interpretable Assumes a linear relationship
Logistic regression Class probability Efficient and explainable Limited with complex boundaries
Decision tree Value or category Easy to visualise Can overfit
Support vector machine Category Effective decision margins Can be expensive at large scale
Neural network Varied Models complex relationships High cost and lower interpretability

Key takeaway

More complex models are not automatically better. The best approach balances predictive performance, interpretability, data availability, development cost and operational risk.

4. Model Development and Evaluation

Model-Development Lifecycle

1. Define

Identify the problem, intended users and success criteria.

2. Prepare

Collect, clean, label and assess the quality of the data.

3. Train

Fit model parameters using the training data.

4. Validate

Compare models and tune settings using validation data.

5. Test

Evaluate final performance using unseen test data.

6. Monitor

Track accuracy, fairness, security and model drift after deployment.

Training, Validation and Testing

Training Set

Used to adjust model parameters and learn relationships from the available examples.

Validation Set

Used to compare approaches, tune hyperparameters and support model selection.

Test Set

Used for final evaluation on data that did not influence model development.

Repeatedly using the test set during development can produce an over-optimistic result because design decisions gradually become adapted to that data. The test set should therefore remain separate until final evaluation, while validation or cross-validation supports model selection (Géron, 2023).

Features and Labels

Features are the input values used by a model. A label is the target output in supervised learning. Feature selection and preparation can strongly affect model performance.

Irrelevant, duplicated or misleading features may reduce accuracy, create unstable predictions or introduce unfair relationships.

Overfitting and Underfitting

Underfitting

The model is too simple to represent important patterns. Performance is poor on both training and unseen data.

Appropriate Fit

The model captures useful patterns and generalises effectively to unseen examples.

Overfitting

The model learns the training examples too closely and performs poorly on new data.

Performance Metrics

Accuracy measures the proportion of correct predictions, but it can be misleading when one class is much more common than another. Metric selection should reflect the operational cost of false positives and false negatives rather than relying on one headline figure (scikit-learn Developers, 2026).

Accuracy

The proportion of all predictions that were correct.

Precision

The proportion of positive predictions that were correct.

Recall

The proportion of actual positive cases that were identified.

F1 Score

A combined measure that balances precision and recall.

Key takeaway

Model development is an iterative lifecycle. Evaluation must include unseen data and metrics selected according to the real consequences of incorrect predictions.

5. AI Ethics and Responsible Development

Ethical AI requires more than high predictive performance. Developers must consider how data is obtained, who may be affected, whether outcomes are fair and how decisions can be challenged.

Data Privacy

AI systems may process personal, behavioural, biometric or location data.

Developers must consider necessity, lawful collection, secure storage, retention and the possibility of re-identification.

Algorithmic Bias

Bias occurs when an AI system produces systematically unfair or unequal outcomes.

It can enter through historical data, sampling, labelling, feature selection, target definitions and deployment decisions.

Transparency

Transparency concerns whether stakeholders can understand how a system was developed, what data it uses and how it is governed.

Explainability

Explainability concerns whether a particular output or decision can be understood.

It is especially important in healthcare, finance, employment and criminal justice.

Accountability

Organisations and professionals remain responsible for AI-related decisions.

Describing an outcome as algorithmic does not remove professional or legal responsibility.

Human Oversight

Appropriate human review should be available where automated decisions can materially affect individuals.

Responsible-AI Controls

  • Data minimisation and privacy protection
  • Representative data assessment
  • Fairness and bias testing
  • Documented model limitations
  • Human review and escalation
  • Security testing
  • Performance monitoring
  • Model-drift detection
  • Audit records
  • Incident-response procedures
  • Processes for challenging decisions
  • Defined accountability

Wider Governance Context

Responsible AI is an approach to designing, deploying and governing AI systems in ways that are lawful, ethical, secure and socially appropriate. UNESCO (2021) places human rights, human dignity, fairness, transparency and environmental responsibility within this wider governance context.

Key takeaway

A technically accurate model may still be inappropriate if its data, deployment context, decision process or social consequences are not responsibly managed.

6. Professional Skills and Employability

Technical Skills

Computing professionals require capability in programming, databases, networking, testing, security and software design.

AI-related roles may also require statistics, data processing, model development and performance evaluation.

Transferable Skills

Employers value communication, teamwork, problem-solving, planning, adaptability and professional judgement.

Technical knowledge has limited value when a professional cannot explain risks, document decisions or work effectively with others.

Professional Responsibility

Professionals must consider security, privacy, ethical consequences, legal requirements and the limitations of the systems they create.

Continuous Professional Development

Computing changes rapidly, so professional development cannot be treated as a one-off activity. BCS (2026) identifies continuing professional development as a means of maintaining competence, recording learning and planning future improvement.

Reflect

Review current skills, experience and areas requiring improvement.

Plan

Set realistic objectives based on career and learning priorities.

Learn

Complete formal study, projects, courses, reading or professional activities.

Apply

Use the learning in practical technical or professional work.

Evidence

Record outputs, feedback, completed work and measurable results.

Review

Evaluate progress and update future development priorities.

SWOT Analysis

Strengths

Internal capabilities and positive attributes that can support progress.

Weaknesses

Internal limitations or skills that require development.

Opportunities

External circumstances that may support learning, employment or career development.

Threats

External risks or barriers that may affect progress.

Personal Development Plans

A personal development plan translates reflection into defined actions. Effective targets should identify the skill to be developed, the evidence required, the support or resources needed and a realistic deadline.

Digital Professional Portfolios

A digital portfolio provides evidence of knowledge, practical projects, reflection and professional development. It can demonstrate capability more clearly than a list of skills alone.

Context

Explain the purpose of the work and the problem being addressed.

Technical Decisions

Explain tools, methods, architecture and key design choices.

Evidence

Include appropriate screenshots, diagrams, testing records and source-code examples.

Reflection

Evaluate challenges, outcomes, limitations and lessons learned.

Key takeaway

Employability requires evidence of both technical competence and professional behaviours. Continuous reflection and structured development help keep those capabilities current.

Overall Evaluation

Artificial intelligence combines technical design with ethical and professional responsibility. A successful AI system must do more than produce accurate outputs. It should use data appropriately, avoid unreasonable discrimination, support meaningful oversight and remain secure and maintainable after deployment.
