Overview

Machine learning approaches differ according to the type of feedback available during training. Supervised learning uses known targets, unsupervised learning identifies structure without target labels, and reinforcement learning learns through interaction and reward (Russell and Norvig, 2021). These categories are useful abstractions, although practical systems may combine more than one learning method.

The most appropriate approach depends on the problem, available data, required level of explanation and cost of incorrect results.

Learning-Type Comparison

Learning Type How It Works Appropriate Applications Limitations
Supervised learning Learns from examples containing input values and known target outputs. Spam detection, credit-risk prediction, medical classification, demand forecasting and image recognition. Requires labelled data, may reproduce historical bias and may fail when future data differs from training data.
Unsupervised learning Finds patterns, groups or structure in data without known target labels. Customer segmentation, document grouping, anomaly detection, exploratory analysis and feature reduction. Results may be difficult to validate, clusters may not have meaningful interpretations and outputs depend strongly on data preparation.
Reinforcement learning An agent learns actions through rewards and penalties received from an environment. Robotics, game-playing systems, dynamic resource allocation, automated control and route optimisation. Can require many interactions, poor reward design may create unintended behaviour and real-world experimentation may be unsafe.

Supervised Learning Evaluation

Supervised learning is appropriate where historical examples contain reliable target values. A financial organisation may train a classification model using past transactions labelled as fraudulent or legitimate.

The advantage is that performance can be measured against known answers. However, the labels may contain errors or reflect past decisions that were unfair. A model trained on those labels may automate the same problems at a larger scale. Evaluation must therefore examine data provenance and group-level outcomes, not only aggregate predictive performance (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

Unsupervised Learning Evaluation

Unsupervised learning is useful when the organisation has data but no agreed target labels. A retailer could group customers according to purchasing behaviour and then examine whether the groups support different marketing strategies.

The method can reveal patterns that were not expected. However, the discovered groups are mathematical structures rather than automatically meaningful business categories. Human interpretation remains necessary, and analysts should test whether alternative features or distance measures produce materially different results.

Reinforcement Learning Evaluation

Reinforcement learning is appropriate for sequential decisions where one action affects future choices. A warehouse robot, for example, must select movements that eventually reach a destination while avoiding obstacles.

The approach can identify strategies that are difficult to program directly. However, a reward function may not represent every real objective. An agent may maximise its numerical reward in a way that is technically successful but operationally undesirable. Safety constraints and human review are therefore necessary where exploration could affect people or physical systems.

Machine Learning Algorithm Comparison

Approach Main Strengths Main Limitations Suitable Applications
Linear regression Simple, efficient and relatively easy to explain. Assumes an approximately linear relationship and can be sensitive to unusual values. Price estimation, demand forecasting and numerical trend analysis.
Logistic regression Efficient classification with probability outputs and interpretable coefficients. May not represent complex non-linear decision boundaries without additional feature work. Binary classification, medical risk analysis and customer-response prediction.
Decision tree Easy to visualise, handles non-linear relationships and requires limited data scaling. Can overfit and may change significantly after small changes to training data. Decision support, eligibility checks, classification and rule discovery.
Support vector machine Effective on moderate-sized datasets and can produce strong separating boundaries. Can be slow on large datasets and less interpretable when complex kernels are used. Text classification, image classification and high-dimensional datasets.
Neural network Can represent highly complex relationships and process images, audio and language. Requires significant data and computing resources and can be difficult to explain. Computer vision, speech recognition, language processing and complex pattern recognition.

Selecting an Approach

A more complex model is not automatically better. A simple model may be preferable when the dataset is small, the decision must be explained or the performance difference is limited. Model choice should be justified by evidence from validation, operational constraints and the risk of error rather than by perceived technical sophistication (Géron, 2023).

Model selection should consider:

  • the size and quality of available data;
  • whether labels exist;
  • the cost of false positives and false negatives;
  • training and operating costs;
  • explainability requirements;
  • privacy and fairness risks;
  • the expected rate of change in the data.

Critical Evaluation

Linear and logistic regression are useful when simplicity and explanation are important. Decision trees provide understandable decisions but require controls against overfitting.

Support vector machines can perform well with high-dimensional information, while neural networks are effective for highly complex data. Their additional complexity may not be justified for every problem.

The final choice should be based on evidence from validation and testing rather than assumptions about which technique is most advanced.
