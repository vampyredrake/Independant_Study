Ethical Issues in Artificial Intelligence

Privacy, Bias, Transparency and Accountability

Critical Discussion

Artificial intelligence can improve efficiency, prediction and access to services, but it also creates ethical risks because automated systems can influence employment, education, finance, healthcare and public services. The impact of an AI system depends not only on technical accuracy but also on how data is collected, how decisions are made and who remains responsible when harm occurs. NIST therefore frames trustworthy AI in terms of validity, safety, resilience, transparency, explainability, privacy and managed harmful bias (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023).

Privacy is a major concern because AI systems often require large datasets. These datasets may include personal, behavioural, biometric or location information. Collecting more data can improve performance, but it may conflict with data-minimisation principles. Consent may also be weak where users do not understand how their data will be reused. Removing direct identifiers is not always sufficient because individuals may be re-identified by combining several attributes. The ICO states that data protection obligations apply throughout the AI lifecycle, including design, training, deployment and monitoring (Information Commissioner's Office, 2023).

Algorithmic bias is another significant issue. Models learn from historical data, and historical decisions may reflect unequal treatment. Bias can also be introduced through incomplete samples, inconsistent labels or the selection of unsuitable features. A system may appear accurate overall while performing poorly for a smaller group. Fairness cannot be achieved only by removing protected characteristics because other variables may act as indirect proxies. Organisations should therefore test outcomes across relevant groups and review whether the model's objective is itself appropriate. Fairness metrics can also conflict, so the chosen definition must be justified in relation to context and potential harm (UNESCO, 2021).

Transparency and explainability are necessary where automated decisions have serious consequences. A user affected by a credit, employment or healthcare decision may reasonably expect to understand the main factors involved. However, advanced models can be difficult to interpret. Simplifying a model may improve explanation while reducing predictive performance. This creates a trade-off that should be considered according to the risk of the application rather than resolved by always choosing the most complex model.

Accountability is essential because responsibility cannot be transferred to an algorithm. Developers, managers, data owners and organisations all make decisions that affect the final system. Clear ownership, documentation, audit trails and human review are required so that harmful outputs can be challenged and corrected. Human oversight must also be meaningful; a person who automatically accepts every recommendation does not provide an effective safeguard. The OECD AI Principles similarly require traceability and accountability for the proper functioning of AI systems (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2024).

Overall, responsible AI requires more than a technical accuracy target. Ethical practice involves privacy by design, fairness evaluation, appropriate explanation, security, monitoring and clear responsibility throughout the system lifecycle. These controls cannot remove every risk, and some applications may remain unacceptable even after mitigation. Responsible governance must therefore include the option not to deploy a system when expected benefits do not justify residual harm.

Ethical Issue Summary

Issue Main Risk Appropriate Response
Privacy Excessive collection, misuse or exposure of personal information. Data minimisation, lawful processing, encryption, access control and retention limits.
Bias Unequal or unfair outcomes for particular individuals or groups. Representative data, group-based testing, human review and continued monitoring.
Transparency Users may not know that AI is being used or how decisions are produced. Clear communication, model documentation and understandable explanations.
Accountability Responsibility becomes unclear when harmful decisions are attributed to software. Named owners, audit records, appeal routes and incident processes.

Main Conclusion

AI ethics should be treated as part of software quality. A system that is accurate but unfair, unaccountable or excessively intrusive should not be considered a successful professional solution.
