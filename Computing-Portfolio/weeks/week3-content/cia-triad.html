Purpose of the CIA Triad

The CIA triad is a foundational model used to classify the security requirements of information and systems. It consists of confidentiality, integrity and availability (Stallings, 2017).

The model supports risk assessment by encouraging an organisation to consider whether information could be disclosed, altered or made unavailable. It is most useful when each principle is linked to specific assets, consequences and measurable controls rather than treated as a generic checklist.

Confidentiality

Confidentiality ensures that information is only available to authorised people, systems or processes.

Example Threats

  • stolen user accounts;
  • misconfigured cloud storage;
  • data interception;
  • insider disclosure;
  • lost devices.

Example Controls

  • encryption;
  • access control;
  • multifactor authentication;
  • data classification;
  • least privilege.

Integrity

Integrity ensures that information remains accurate, complete and protected from unauthorised modification.

Example Threats

  • SQL injection;
  • malicious software updates;
  • unauthorised database changes;
  • accidental data corruption;
  • tampered audit records.

Example Controls

  • hashing;
  • digital signatures;
  • database constraints;
  • change control;
  • audit logging.

Availability

Availability ensures that systems and information are accessible to authorised users when required.

Example Threats

  • DDoS attacks;
  • ransomware;
  • hardware failure;
  • power failure;
  • cloud-service disruption.

Example Controls

  • backups;
  • redundancy;
  • load balancing;
  • capacity planning;
  • incident-response procedures.

Applying the Model

Example: Online Banking

Confidentiality requires protection of account details, personal information and transaction records. Encryption, secure authentication and restricted staff access are important controls.

Integrity requires balances and transactions to remain accurate. Database transactions, validation, audit logs and separation of duties reduce the risk of unauthorised changes.

Availability requires customers to access services when needed. Redundant systems, DDoS protection, backups and tested recovery arrangements support continuity.

Example: Healthcare System

Confidentiality protects sensitive medical information. Integrity ensures that clinicians receive correct records and medication details. Availability is critical because unavailable systems may delay treatment.

The example demonstrates that all three principles can be essential at the same time. Focusing on one principle while neglecting another can create serious risk. In clinical systems, for example, excessively restrictive access may protect confidentiality while delaying time-critical treatment, so controls require proportionate risk assessment.

Security Trade-Offs

Security controls can affect more than one part of the triad. A strict access policy may improve confidentiality and integrity but reduce availability if authorised users cannot obtain information quickly.

Redundant copies improve availability but create more locations where confidential information must be protected. Encryption improves confidentiality but can affect recovery if keys are lost.

Organisations should therefore evaluate controls as part of a complete system rather than assuming that every control produces only positive effects. The NIST Cybersecurity Framework similarly emphasises coordinated governance and risk management rather than isolated technical measures (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024).

Limitations of the CIA Triad

The CIA triad is a useful starting point, but it does not represent every security objective in detail. Additional concerns can include authenticity, accountability, privacy, non-repudiation and safety.

Authenticity concerns whether an identity, message or system is genuine. Accountability concerns whether actions can be traced to a responsible entity. Privacy concerns the fair and lawful handling of personal information.

These concerns relate to the CIA principles but may require separate analysis. The triad should therefore support a wider risk-management process rather than replace it. In particular, the model does not by itself determine risk appetite, legal obligations, human safety requirements or the effectiveness of implemented controls.

Overall Evaluation

The CIA triad gives organisations a clear way to identify the types of harm that may affect information. It is particularly useful during risk assessment and system design.

Its main value is that it prevents security from being reduced to secrecy alone. A system with strong confidentiality can still fail if its data is inaccurate or its services are unavailable.
